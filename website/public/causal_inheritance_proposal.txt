= Summary

Distributed language-model systems make questions of identity and continuity experimentally manipulable. Consider several weight-tied model copies that run independently, maintain different local histories, and exchange information over an asynchronous communication bus. Shared weights give the copies a common type, but not a common numerical state; the bus gives the collective a causal history that can survive delay, node replacement, partition, and recombination.

This proposal asks a deliberately operational question: *what features of such a system carry stable roles, commitments, and behavior across disruption?* The working hypothesis is that continuity follows *causal inheritance*---the live process by which later states inherit local memory, mailbox contents, commitments, and effects from earlier states---more strongly than it follows hardware identity, network address, or instantaneous state similarity.

The goal is not to settle metaphysical personhood. It is to make competing notions of continuity produce different experimental predictions.

= Experimental Substrate

The substrate is a trained distributed LLM collective such as the Brain Gates architecture: multiple copies share model weights but receive different observations and maintain separate recurrent or contextual state. Copies exchange bounded messages asynchronously. Communication may be delayed, dropped, reordered, or interrupted, and nodes may enter or leave during an episode.

Three descriptive levels coexist:

- the *shared type*: common model parameters and communication protocol;
- the *local process*: one copy's observation history, internal state, mailbox, and causal future;
- the *collective process*: the copies plus their live communication history when successful behavior depends on information propagating through the bus.

These levels need not be forced in advance into "one mind" or "many agents." The experiment instead asks which level best predicts what persists under intervention.

= Competing Continuity Hypotheses

Several hypotheses make separable predictions:

- *Node identity:* roles and commitments attach primarily to a physical process or machine.
- *Address identity:* they attach to stable sender identifiers or positions in the communication topology.
- *Local-state identity:* they follow the copy that inherits a particular local memory and mailbox.
- *Pattern identity:* sufficiently similar instantaneous global states count as continuations regardless of causal history.
- *Causal inheritance:* continuity follows the ongoing chain by which each new state is produced from prior local and collective state.
- *Collective organization:* some properties belong principally to the bus-connected process and survive replacement of any particular node so long as the relevant causal organization remains intact.

The last two can overlap. The point is to measure when they predict behavior better than simpler node- or address-based accounts.

= Interventions

The architecture makes causal inheritance directly manipulable. Candidate interventions include:

1. *Pseudonymize senders.* Remove stable sender identity while preserving message content and timing.
2. *Reassign addresses.* Swap node identifiers mid-episode without moving local state.
3. *Replace a node.* Substitute a fresh copy while either preserving or dropping its local state, mailbox, and outstanding commitments.
4. *State transplant.* Move one node's local state and mailbox onto different hardware or a newly instantiated process.
5. *Bus partition.* Split the collective into disconnected groups, allow them to evolve independently, then reconnect them.
6. *Collective fork.* Clone the complete collective state and let both descendants diverge under different observations.
7. *Bus split.* Divide one trained collective across two independent buses without cloning all local state.

The fork is especially diagnostic. If two exact copies of the complete state diverge, instantaneous pattern similarity cannot by itself identify a unique continuing original; both branches share the same past and acquire distinct causal futures.

= Measurements

After each intervention, measure what follows the hardware, address, local state, and communication history. Useful dependent variables include:

- task performance and policy choices;
- persistence of previously established commitments or plans;
- recovery of distributed facts known only through earlier communication;
- role behavior under sender pseudonymization or reassignment;
- sensitivity to loss of mailbox versus loss of hardware identity;
- divergence after collective forks;
- recovery after partition and reconnection.

On sparse mixture-of-experts backbones, per-copy expert-routing histograms provide one quantitative fingerprint of situated computational role. Additional probes can compare activation statistics, message distributions, tool-use patterns, and causal influence on later collective decisions.

No single fingerprint should be treated as identity. The question is which variables predict preserved behavior across controlled transformations.

= Identity-Neutral Training

Training tasks should remain identity-neutral. A system explicitly trained to call itself a collective mind, to preserve named personas, or to report continuity will largely reproduce the evaluator's framing.

Continuity probes should therefore be introduced after the cooperative protocol has been learned. Self-reports, if collected, should be compared against behavioral invariants rather than treated as ground truth. The strongest evidence is causal: which interventions preserve or destroy the competencies, commitments, and role structure already present before the intervention?

= Operational Closure

A useful communication bus is not by itself evidence for a larger self. A stronger criterion is *operational closure*: information acquired in one stream reliably alters the future policy of the whole; commitments survive movement between nodes; and no fixed node is the sole bearer of the collective's relevant history.

Complementary roles among weight-tied copies demonstrate situated differentiation, not automatically separate selves. Conversely, shared weights do not collapse independently evolving copies into one individual. What matters experimentally is where the system's causal dependencies actually run.

= Predictions

If causal inheritance is the strongest account of continuity, the following pattern should emerge:

- changing hardware or network address while preserving local state and mailbox has little effect;
- dropping inherited state during replacement causes a larger discontinuity than replacing the process that carries it;
- stable roles can survive sender pseudonymization when their causal inputs and local histories are preserved;
- a partition creates increasingly independent descendants as causal interaction is removed;
- reconnecting partitions may restore coordination but should not erase divergence accumulated while separated;
- a complete fork produces two equally continuous descendants rather than one privileged original;
- collective properties survive turnover of individual nodes when the communication-and-memory organization needed to realize them remains causally connected.

Failure of these predictions would be informative. For example, if roles rigidly follow addresses after repeated state transplants, address identity would be doing more causal work than the hypothesis expects.

= Research Question

#quote(block: true)[
When a distributed language-model collective is copied, interrupted, partitioned, or rebuilt, what actually carries its learned roles and commitments forward: the node, the address, the instantaneous pattern, the local memory, or the ongoing causal history?
]

The working hypothesis is deliberately narrower than a claim about consciousness or personhood: *continuity attaches most strongly to the live causal organization by which each next state inherits from the last.* Distributed model architectures make that claim testable.
